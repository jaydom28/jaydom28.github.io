Welcome to my crash course on learning Khmer. If you're here, hopefully means that you are interested in learning the Khmer language. In this chapter we will go over the basics of how the Khmer writing system works.

## The Khmer Writing System

The Khmer writing system is significantly different from English, but it can be learned with consistent practice no matter what age you start at. In English, we have a bunch of letters and they all come one after another. Their pronunciations can change depending on surrounding letters. Linguists refer to this writing system as an **Alphabet**. Note however, that in this context, **Alphabet** refers to the writing system that English uses and **not** the set of Latin script (ABCDEFG...).

The **writing systems** used by the most widely known languages are:
- Alphabets (English, Spanish, Russian)
- Logographs (Chinese, Japanese Kanji)
- Syllabaries (Korean)
- Abugidas (Khmer, Thai, Laos, Hindi)

The Khmer writing system is called an **abugida**. This means that instead of a linear sequence of letters read from left to right, there are consonants and sometimes they have vowels or even other consonants attached to them. Depending on the vowel, it can sit before, after, above, or below a consonant which can be confusing or even intimidating at first. But spending quality time with the language without judging yourself will naturally improve your ability to recognize them.

### What Is A Consonant's Built-In Vowel Sound?

Every consonant has a **built-in vowel sound**, and the vowel's job is to modify this sound. Namely, every consonant in Khmer is either an **-aw** consonant or an **-or** consonant (which your ears might hear as just "o" if you hear a Khmer speaker say it). Here are some examples:

ស - saw
ក - kaw
អ - aw
រ - ror (you'll hear someone call it ro)
វ - vor (you'll hear someone call it vo)
ល - lor (you'll hear someone call it lo)

The built-in vowel sound is relevant because it actually affects how the vowels interact with the consonant (daunting, I know). This means that **some** vowels in Khmer have **two possible sounds** depending on if they're attached to an **-aw** consonant or an **-or** consonant. If you think that's too much of a hassle, imagine what Chinese and Japanese speakers have to go through.

### Vowels Attached To Consonants

The purpose of a vowel is just to overwrite the built-in consonant vowel sound. Let's look at the following vowel:

ា(ah)
: when this vowel is attached to an **-aw** consonant, it will give it an **-ah** sound, however when attached to an **-or** consonant, it will give an **-ie** sound. Most Khmer computer fonts will render vowels with a circle, indicating where the consonant should go, but someone writing the vowel by hand wouldn't also write this circle

```text
ស(saw) + ា(ah) = សា(sah: not a real word, just demonstration)
ប(baw) + ា(ah) + ទ(thor) = បាទ(baht: yes for males)
```

Here is an example with **-or** consonants, notice the same vowel produces a different sound:

```text
ទ(thor) + ា(ah) = ទា(thie: duck 🦆)
វ(vor) + ា(ah) = វា(vie: pronoun meaning "it")
```

### Sub-consonants Attached To Consonants

In the way that Khmer vowels modify a consonant's built-in vowel sound, sub-consonants modify a consonant's base sound. Every consonant has a corresponding sub-consonant form. Luckily a sub-consonant's sound is not dependent on the type of consonant it is applied to. Here are some examples using the sub-consonant ្រ(ror) whose regular form is រ(ror):

្រ(ror)
: when this sub-consonant is applied to a consonant, it modifies the sound by appending the "r" sound to the consonant base sound

```text
ស(saw) + ្រ(-ror) = ស្រ(sraw: not a word, just demonstration)
ត(thaw) + ្រ(-ror) + ី(-ey: vowel) = ត្រី(threy: fish 🐟)
```

All our examples so far have been single syllables, but words can be multiple syllables. Khmer also has no spaces, which means one has to recognize where words begin and end, which becomes easier with time.
