Here we'll go over basic pronunciation rules and sentence syntax.

## Stressing Syllables

As English speakers, we're able to intuit what syllable needs to be stressed when speaking a word. For example:

- **Laugh**able is pronounced LAUGH-a-ble
- In**du**bitably is pronounced in-DU-bi-ta-bly
- Seren**di**pity is pronounced se-ren-DI-pi-ty

These are things we don't really think about. It's hard to give concrete rules for what syllable to stress, but we can all agree that it sounds very weird when the wrong syllable is stressed. Khmer however has a really simple rule for what syllable to stress: **the last syllable of a word is almost always stressed in Khmer**. Then if there is a compound word or phrase, the last syllable of each individual word is stressed. For example:

ភាសា (pie-**SAA**)
: language

និយាយ (ne-**YIEY**)
: to speak
: If you're Khmer-American, you'll notice that when reading the Khmer, the pronunciation should be **yiey**. When spoken, it sounds kind of like **yey**. Khmer people do say **yiey** but it's often heard as **yey**. Take away the **ne** part, and you get the word for grandma, **យាយ**.

លេខាធិការ (lay-ka-te-**KAA**)
: secretary

## Pronouns in Khmer

<span class="pronoun">ខ្ញុំ(kyom)</span>
: I

<span class="pronoun">អ្នក(neak)</span>
: you

<span class="pronoun">គាត់, នាង, វា (koat, nieng, vie)</span>
: third person singulars: he, she, it
: Even though <span class="pronoun">នាង</span> means "she", most Khmer people will use <span class="pronoun">គាត់</span> regardless of whether they are saying he or she. So even if you won't be using <span class="pronoun">នាង</span>, it's good to know it exists.
: <span class="pronoun">វា</span> is used for inanimate objects and animals. It is rude to use it for people.
: If you're a Khmer heritage speaker, you're probably used to hearing something like **wie** for the word **it** but Khmer spelling clearly shows **vie**. Khmer speakers tend to use the w and v sounds interchangeably. Thai does the [same thing](https://modulolotus.net/blog/thai-v-vs-w/) and it's called a [voiced labiodental approximant.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_labiodental_approximant) But for consistency, any romanization of Khmer in these blogs will follow what the sound **should be** based on the Khmer spelling.

<span class="pronoun">ពួកយើង or យើង  (puok yeung or yeung)</span>
: we

<span class="pronoun">អ្នកទាំងអស់គ្នា (Neak teang aws knie)</span>
: plural form of you, also can be understood as **you all**

<span class="pronoun">ពួកគេ or គេ (puok kei or kei)</span>
: they

## Sentence Word Order

In Khmer the sentence word order is intuitive. It is a <span class="pronoun">subject</span>-<span class="verb">verb</span>-<span class="direct-obj">object</span>( or **SVO**) language. The example below illustrates the word order.

<span class="pronoun">I</span> <span class="verb">drink</span> <span class="direct-obj">beer</span>
: **Khmer:** <span class="pronoun">ខ្ញុំ</span><span class="verb">ផឹក</span><span class="direct-obj">បៀរ</span>
: **Khmer:** <span class="pronoun">kyom</span><span class="verb">puk</span><span class="direct-obj">bier</span>
: **Japanese:** <span class="pronoun">私は</span><span class="direct-obj">ビールを</span><span class="verb">飲みます</span>
: **Japanese:** <span class="pronoun">watashi-wa</span> <span class="direct-obj">biru-wo</span> <span class="verb">nomimasu</span>

In the example above, it's clear not all languages are SVO. Some are SOV and there are languages that use everything in between. Seeing how other languages work at a high level helps you to better visualize what your target language makes easier or harder. Overall it helps you gain an appreciation for your target language.
